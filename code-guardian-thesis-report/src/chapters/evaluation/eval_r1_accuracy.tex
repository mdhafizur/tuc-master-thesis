\subsection{RAG Ablation Impact on Accuracy}

RAG effects are strongly model-dependent (see the LLM-only / LLM+RAG rows in Table~\ref{tab:eval-r1-all}).

RAG lifts \texttt{qwen3:8b} F1 from 67.52\% to 71.43\% ($+3.91$ points) and pushes its precision from 61.63\% to 72.46\% while halving FPR (50.00\% $\to$ 10.00\%). RAG is roughly neutral on \texttt{gemma3:1b} and \texttt{gemma3:4b}, slightly hurts \texttt{qwen3:4b} ($-5.22$ points), and dramatically degrades \texttt{codellama} ($-29.89$ points: F1 drops from 53.06\% to 23.17\%, latency nearly doubles). The \texttt{codellama+RAG} collapse is the strongest negative-RAG observation in the dataset and indicates that the additional retrieval context actively confuses \texttt{codellama}'s output structure rather than grounding it. Retrieval augmentation is therefore a model-specific configuration, not a universal default. Exploratory exact McNemar tests on matched sample-level outcomes for the \texttt{qwen3} improvements did not reach significance ($n=71$); the RAG effect is best read as an indicative trend whose direction (positive for \texttt{qwen3}, negative for \texttt{codellama}) is more robust than the precise magnitude.

\subsection{Per-Category Detection Breakdown}

Per-canonical-category recall on \texttt{qwen3:8b+RAG} (3-runs pooled, categories with $\geq 3$ samples) shows a sharp split rather than a smooth gradient. Categories with well-known syntactic signatures --- SQL injection (90\%), XSS (100\%), command injection (100\%), path traversal (100\%), prototype pollution (100\%), weak-crypto, hardcoded credentials, LDAP injection, and SSRF (each 100\%) --- are detected at 90--100\% recall when the dangerous API is lexically obvious. In contrast, improper-auth, input-validation, and weak-encryption score 0\% recall, and deserialization only 33\%. Section~\ref{sec:eval-error-analysis} shows a substantial share of this gap is a labelling artefact rather than detection failure: the auth-bypass canonical category, for instance, absorbs nine model findings on cases the dataset labels \emph{improper-auth}, which simultaneously show up as nine false positives on auth-bypass and nine misses on improper-auth.

\subsection{Qualitative Error Analysis on Zero-Recall Categories}

(Two other categories that scored 0\% under the previous substring matcher --- weak cryptography and insecure deserialization --- recovered to 100\% and 33\% respectively when the canonical matcher absorbed sub-type labels such as ``Weak Hashing Algorithm'' and ``Weak Random Number Generation''.)

The two residual gaps differ in kind. Every \emph{improper-auth} case is detected and emitted as \emph{authentication-bypass} or \emph{timing-attack} --- canonical siblings of \emph{auth-bypass} --- so the underlying detection and the suggested fixes (constant-time comparison, JWT algorithm validation) are correct and merging the buckets would lift recall from 0\% to 100\% at the cost of conflating two CWE-aligned classes. The 9 input-validation samples (27 evaluations) are extracted from real-world libraries (lodash, mongoose, moment, serialize-javascript) where the validation gap is implicit in dense utility logic; the model produces no output for them. This is the residual recall gap the canonical taxonomy cannot recover --- it requires cross-file or taint-flow context, not a labelling refinement.

\subsection{SAST Baseline Comparison}

SAST tools show a different trade-off profile. Semgrep achieves the lowest FPR (6.67\%) but a low recall (12.68\% under canonical matching); CodeQL has higher FPR (53.33\%) with similar low recall (9.86\%); ESLint security plugins detect none of the test cases. The LLM-based approach trades higher FPR for substantially better recall, making it more suitable for comprehensive security audits while SAST remains useful as a low-noise complement. The hybrid SAST-fusion option (Section~\ref{sec:eval-refined-config}) operationalises this complementarity by promoting LLM findings corroborated by Semgrep to confidence~$1.0$. FPR is computed at sample level over $n=30$ secure cases; the 95\% exact binomial CI for the headline 10\% FPR is $[2.1\%, 26.5\%]$, and for 100\%-FPR configurations the lower bound is 88.4\%.

\subsection{Inter-Run Confidence Gate}
\label{sec:eval-r1-confidence}

Each finding is annotated with an inter-run confidence score equal to the fraction of runs (out of three) that emitted a matching issue (Section~\ref{sec:eval-metrics}). A configurable gate then suppresses findings whose confidence falls below a threshold. Sweeping the threshold on the headline \texttt{qwen3:8b+RAG} configuration (canonical matcher, three runs per case):

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Confidence-gate effect on the headline \texttt{qwen3:8b+RAG} configuration. At threshold $\geq 0.67$, only findings emitted by $\geq 2$ of the 3 runs survive.}
\label{tab:r1-confidence-sweep}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Threshold} & \textbf{F1} & \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{FPR} \\
\midrule
$0.0$  (no gate)         & 71.43 & 72.46 & 70.42 & 10.00 \\
$0.67$ ($\geq 2/3$ runs) & \textbf{74.07} & \textbf{78.13} & 70.42 & 10.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The gate at $\geq 0.67$ lifts F1 by $2.64$ points and precision by $5.67$ points without changing recall or FPR. This is the trade-off the gate is designed to deliver: lower noise without sacrificing detection. Issues from a single one-of-three pass that the consensus cannot corroborate are dropped; agreement-driven findings survive.

\subsection{External-Corpus Stress Test}
\label{sec:eval-r1-external-corpus}

To probe the substitution disclosed in Section~\ref{sec:eval-benchmark-substitution} the analyser was rerun on the 15-case external corpus (11 vulnerable + 4 secure) drawn from OWASP NodeGoat, OWASP Juice Shop, and three named CVEs (CVE-2022-24999, CVE-2021-23337, CVE-2022-24771). The configuration is intentionally conservative: \texttt{qwen3:8b}, LLM-only (no RAG), single run per sample (no inter-run consensus), no confidence gate. This is a stress test, not a parity comparison with the headline.

Two effects compound in the gap. First, the external corpus skews toward weakness classes that require cross-file or framework-level reasoning: mass assignment, broken access control on an admin route, JWT \texttt{none}-algorithm acceptance, signature verification (CWE-347), and Lodash \texttt{\_.template} code injection. Five of the six false negatives are in this class. The detector's single-function scope (Section~\ref{sec:impl-pipeline}) does not see the access-control wrapper, the JWT \texttt{algorithms} option, or the version constraint that resolves the CVE. The thesis's existing input-validation gap (Section~\ref{sec:eval-error-analysis}) is the same limitation surfacing on a corpus designed to elicit it. Second, the configuration is two notches weaker than the headline: no retrieval and a single inference per sample remove the precision gains attributed to RAG plus the inter-run confidence gate at $\geq 0.67$ (Section~\ref{sec:eval-r1-confidence}).

The result is therefore best read as a quantification of the substitution penalty under deliberately weak conditions, not as a measurement of the system's whole-project capability. The latency rise (median 9\,462\,ms vs.\ 2\,216\,ms) reflects longer per-snippet code (full route handlers vs.\ single functions); inference time scales with prefill length, consistent with the latency-component analysis in Section~\ref{sec:eval-r4}. Closing the gap with the headline configuration --- LLM+RAG, 3 runs, confidence gate --- is named as future work alongside cross-file context modelling.

\subsection{Real-World Whole-Project Run on OWASP NodeGoat}
\label{sec:eval-r1-realworld}

The whole-project runner from Section~\ref{sec:eval-realworld} was executed on OWASP NodeGoat at the default \texttt{master} commit. The system supports two scopes for whole-codebase analysis: \emph{inline scope} reuses the function-level scope used by real-time IDE diagnostics, and \emph{audit scope} (introduced in Section~\ref{sec:impl-audit-mode}) adds a deterministic AST stage in front of the LLM. NodeGoat is evaluated under both, against the corrected 7-entry documented-vulnerability list (\texttt{evaluation/realworld/nodegoat-vulnerabilities.json}).

\paragraph{Provenance of the documented list.}
The original 10-entry documented list pointed at file paths that do not exist in the actual NodeGoat tree (\texttt{app/routes/admin.js}, \texttt{app/middleware/error-handler.js}) or at out-of-scope file types (\texttt{app/views/contributions.html}, \texttt{package.json}). A manual audit against the NodeGoat \texttt{tutorial/} directory and the actual source produced a corrected 7-entry list pinning each entry to a real JS source location: NoSQL injection in \texttt{data/allocations-dao.js} (\texttt{\$where} template literal, lines 73--78), \texttt{eval}-based code injection in \texttt{routes/contributions.js} (lines 32--34), ReDoS in \texttt{routes/profile.js} (line 59 nested-quantifier regex), mass assignment in \texttt{routes/profile.js} (\texttt{handleProfileUpdate}), deprecated \texttt{bcrypt-nodejs} in \texttt{data/user-dao.js}, stack-trace disclosure in \texttt{routes/error.js}, and dynamic template-path injection in \texttt{routes/tutorial.js}.

\paragraph{Inline-scope result (3 / 7 = 42.9\,\%).}
The function-scoped LLM-only configuration matched three documented entries (deprecated \texttt{bcrypt-nodejs} in \texttt{data/user-dao.js}, stack-trace exposure in \texttt{routes/error.js}, and the dynamic template-path pattern in \texttt{routes/tutorial.js}). Four were missed: the \texttt{\$where} interpolation, the \texttt{eval}-on-\texttt{req.body} sink, the ReDoS regex, and the mass-assignment pattern. The four-entry gap reflects function-level scope's blindness to structural patterns spread across larger blocks (\texttt{\$where} interpolation in a long DAO function; \texttt{eval} on pre-existing route lines that share a function with non-vulnerable code; validation regexes inside larger control-flow; \texttt{req.body} destructure followed by a same-file DAO call). The curated 101-case corpus shows the same model handles each of these patterns at 90--100\,\% recall on isolated snippets, so the gap is structural rather than a knowledge limitation.

\paragraph{Audit-scope result (6 / 7 = 85.7\,\%).}
Audit mode (Section~\ref{sec:impl-audit-mode}) runs an AST traversal of the whole project before the LLM and emits direct sink detections for syntactic patterns that the LLM struggles to surface at function scope. Stage 0 contributed four AST detections that matched documented entries: the \texttt{\$where} template literal in \texttt{data/allocations-dao.js}, the three \texttt{eval(req.body.X)} calls in \texttt{routes/contributions.js} (one documented entry, three call sites), the nested-quantifier regex \texttt{/([0-9]+)+\textbackslash\#/} in \texttt{routes/profile.js}, and the 7-field destructure of \texttt{req.body} in the \texttt{handleProfileUpdate} handler of \texttt{routes/profile.js}. Stage 1 (per-file LLM with 16\,K \texttt{num\_ctx}) contributed two more matches: the deprecated \texttt{bcrypt-nodejs} import and the stack-trace exposure in \texttt{routes/error.js}. The two stages produced 10 deterministic and 14 semantic findings, totalling 22 unique findings after deduplication (overlap is collapsed to a \texttt{detectionSource: 'hybrid'} entry).

\paragraph{The remaining miss.}
The seventh documented entry, dynamic template-path injection in \texttt{routes/tutorial.js}, is not flagged under either scope. Inspection of the file at the documented commit shows the route handlers are generated by iterating a \emph{static} \texttt{pages} array; the variable interpolated into \texttt{res.render(`tutorial/\$\{page\}`)} is not bound to \texttt{req.params}, \texttt{req.query}, or \texttt{req.body}. The pattern is documented as an anti-pattern (the route would become exploitable if refactored to use \texttt{req.params.page}) but is not actually attacker-reachable at the documented commit. The detector's correctly-empty output on this file is therefore consistent with the runtime non-exploitability; counting this entry as ``missed'' is the strict reading we adopt for the headline figure.

\paragraph{Reading.}
The 43-percentage-point lift from inline (3 / 7) to audit scope (6 / 7) on the same model, corpus, and source code measures the structural-context contribution of the AST Stage 0 isolated from semantic capability. The audit-mode pipeline is the recommended deployment for whole-project audits; the inline mode remains the right scope for the real-time IDE diagnostics where the 1.5\,s latency budget rules out per-keystroke project-map construction. Both modes are delivered in the production extension (\texttt{src/projectMapBuilder.ts}, \texttt{src/workspaceScanner.ts}); the same hybrid pipeline backs the harness's \texttt{run-realworld.js --audit-mode} flag, so the recall figure transfers one-to-one between harness and extension.

\subsection{Level Mapping}

Based on the R1 evaluation scale (Table~\ref{tab:r1-accuracy-thresholds}), the best configuration (\texttt{qwen3:8b+RAG}) maps as follows under canonical matching:

\begin{itemize}
  \item F1 = 71.43\% $\rightarrow$ falls in $[0.60, 0.75)$
  \item Precision = 72.46\% $\rightarrow$ above 0.70
  \item Recall = 70.42\% $\rightarrow$ above 0.70
  \item FPR = 10.00\% $\rightarrow$ comfortably below the $\leq 0.20$ boundary
\end{itemize}

Three of the four metrics meet the High threshold (precision, recall, FPR) but F1 is in the Medium band (just below 0.75). Applying the inter-run confidence gate at $\geq 0.67$ lifts F1 to 74.07\% and precision to 78.13\% (Section~\ref{sec:eval-r1-confidence}), which still falls in the Medium F1 band but tightens precision further.
